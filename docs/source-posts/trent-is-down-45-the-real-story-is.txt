Now layer the 
foreshock mental model
 on top, which I shared in my last KPIT post.
The core rule of that mental model is: 
never buy after the first slowdown.
 Because just like an earthquake, the first tremor is never the real quake, it is only the warning before it. And a slowdown works the same way, the first crack is almost never the last, more are coming behind it.
So ask yourself: is this the first slowdown, or have there been many? This is how you get trapped by the ticker state, and how one can be saved.
The answer is no. The first slowdown started in late 2024 when the stock was at peak valuations, and this is the 6th or 7th slowdown.
And everyone who bought it after the first slowdown, the first crack from 5000, is trapped. That’s the lesson.
Don’t buy just because a stock has dragged 15 percent and looks cheap. And make it a default rule to never buy after the first slowdown of any company, because it might rebound in the short term but eventually it collapses.
This one has dragged 45 percent and it’s still not cheap. It’s still trading at 90 multiples.
And here’s the part nobody wants to name. This valuation is being held up by DIIs blindly buying with hard-earned retail money flowing in through SIPs.
See how the FIIs started dumping it in September 2024, when FII holding was 26 percent and DII was 13 percent. That was the first slowdown.
FII moved from 26 to 15, and DII moved from 13 to 22, the complete opposite. The DIIs handed the FIIs a clean exit, absorbing all their selling at such a high premium, and 
not with their own money but with retail SIP money.
I just don’t know how blind, or how unethical, you have to be to not see valuation excesses like this.
And if you want to see how stretched this really is, look at Lululemon, a better brand that was trading at one point at insane valuations, and see how the reversion happened.
It’s now trading at 9 multiples. Everyone can take Lululemon or any fashion brand as a case study to understand how the underlying model actually operates.
Imagine getting Lululemon at 9 multiples and a market cap of 13 billion, which is less than Trent, which is close to a 20 billion dollar company trading at 90 multiples.
Note: I’m not suggesting anyone buy Lululemon. I’m just showing you the excess. And coming to the reinvestment runway, Lululemon still has all of China, all of India, and almost 90 percent of nations left to penetrate.
Trent is not a floating model. So if anyone has a doubt about the reinvestment engine, they should think along this line.
And always remember: a 100 multiple on a 20-30k crore market cap is very different from a 100 multiple on a 3 lakh crore market cap, because the law of large numbers comes into play.
You might be saved for paying 100 on a 20k crore market cap, but when you pay those multiples on a 2-3 lakh crore market cap, you are looking at lost decades if the EPS engine slows down even a little.
Because if the stock drags 80 percent, you need a 400 percent move to just break even. And that takes a long time for the EPS engine to make happen if it’s operating on a higher base.
You only break even when the engine is powerful enough. Otherwise you are dead, just like the investors of Suzlon for almost 2 decades. And the same will play out with investors in the railways and defence sectors.
Now coming back again to Trent, the growth right now is an illusion of store expansion. I’m pretty confident 60 to 70 percent of those stores will turn out to be bad investments.
Same-store growth will collapse after a point, and it has already started in old stores, but it’s masked by new store additions. It happens because they’re selling the lowest quality product wrapped as a brand. That structure doesn’t survive long, especially in Indian society.
Buying 10 clothes from Zudio that won’t survive 2 washes vs buying one from Uniqlo is the exact same thing as buying 10 low-quality garbage stocks vs owning one quality compounding machine that lasts and compounds for decades.
